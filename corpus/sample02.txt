Azan Fakir, Sufi Literature and Assam: A Historical Discourse

Abstract: 
Sufism is a mystical branch of Islam, characterized by a deep personal experience of the divine. It has evolved through diverse practices, including the recitation of devotional poetry, the whirling dance (like the Sema ceremony of the Whirling Dervishes), and various forms of meditation and spiritual discipline. Sufis are considered Muslim spiritualists who strive to guide others towards a more profound understanding and experience of Islamic teachings. By the 12th century, Sufism had gained significant influence within the Islamic world, with its teachings accessible to a wide range of Muslims. Many Sufi masters transcended racial and religious boundaries, emphasizing universal human values and striving for the betterment of all humanity.Both in India and across many Islamic nations, Sufism has played a vital role in shaping the religious sentiments and beliefs of a vast segment of society, providing a framework for spiritual growth and a deeper connection to the divine.
Zikir is a core practice within Sufism, involving the repetitive recitation of divine names, phrases, or verses as a means of cultivating spiritual awareness and deepening one's connection with God. In Assam, Zikir often incorporates Assamese idioms, reflecting the influence of Assamese Vaishnava literature on the local Sufi tradition. These idioms serve to convey Islamic teachings within a framework familiar to the Assamese people, drawing parallels between Sufi concepts and the language and imagery of Vaishnava literature. 
Keywords : Azan Fakir, Zikir, Sufism, Assamese Vaishnava literature, 17th century Assam, Baul
Introduction 
Islam encompasses a rich mystical tradition known as Sufism. Sufi practitioners explore and deepen their understanding of Islam through diverse spiritual and artistic practices. These may include the recitation of devotional poetry, the whirling dance (like the famous Sema ceremony of the Whirling Dervishes), and various forms of spiritual discipline and meditation. Sufis are considered Muslim spiritualists who strive to guide others towards a more profound comprehension and experience of Islamic teachings. Sufism, a mystical branch of Islam, has profoundly shaped the religious and cultural landscape of India for centuries. Emphasizing inner spiritual transformation through practices like meditation, chanting (zikir), and devotional music, Sufism diverged from strict adherence to religious orthodoxy. Key tenets of Sufism include love for humanity, compassion, and service to others, fostering a spirit of inclusivity and tolerance that transcended religious boundaries.   
Sufi saints played a crucial role in promoting social harmony and interfaith dialogue. They often engaged in social reform, advocating for the rights of the marginalized and fostering understanding between different communities. This emphasis on human values resonated deeply with the Indian ethos, leading to a fruitful exchange of ideas and a rich cultural synthesis. Sufi saints and their teachings inspired countless individuals, irrespective of their religious affiliations, leaving an enduring legacy of love, compassion, and spiritual unity.   
The influence of Sufism can be seen in various facets of Indian culture, including music, poetry, art, and architecture.
Sufi shrines and khanqahs (spiritual centers) became important centers of cultural and social activity, attracting people from all walks of life. The teachings and practices of prominent Sufi saints like Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti and Nizamuddin Auliya continue to inspire and guide seekers of truth across India, reminding us of the importance of love, compassion, and the pursuit of spiritual enlightenment.

Sufism had grown in importance in the Islamic world by the twelfth century, and its teachings had reached all Muslims. There were also a significant number of Sufis who stood above religious and racial divides to advance the interests of all humankind. Overall, Sufism offered the religious sentiments and beliefs of a large cross-section of society purpose and meaning, both in India and in many Islamic countries .
Assam witnessed growth of Sufism alongside the rest of the globe and the nation. The Sufi literature was initially created by Sufi scholars and saints in Arabic, followed by Persian, and then in other regional and local languages in later times. The intention was to influence the local population in favour of Sufi culture. The 17thcentury Sufi saint from Assam, Azan Fakir, is most recognised for his devotional Zikir songs that he performed in Assamese, a language that was foreign to him.These songs have been kept alive by oral tradition that have been passed down from one generation to the other. In the 1950s, Gauhati University entrusted Syed Abdul Malik, a litterateur of repute to collect the Zikirsand document them. A book titled AsamiyaZikirAru Zari was eventually released by Malik in 1958.Zari is primarily based on the tragedy of Karbala and is most usually performed during the yearly Muhharam festival; the practise of Zari in Assam is still unclear though.
Statement of the problem:
The advent of Sufism in Assam and the contribution of the Sufi saints like Azan Fakir  in the field of Assamese literature, culture and society have not been brought under the light of academic discussion so far. The present work is a modest attempt to explore the importance of the contributions of Azan Fakir in this direction.
Literature Review:
Aquil Hussain in his work Sufism and Society in Medieval India brings together significant studies on the ongoing discord about the various roles played by Sufis in mediaeval Indian society and culture. The study focuses on important subjects including Sufis' exchanges or encounters in the Indian setting, episodic conversion, the Islamization process, and the growth of Islam in India. Popular social historians including Khaliq Ahmad Nizami, Aziz Ahmad, S.A.A. Rizvi, Richard M. Eaton, Carl W. Ernst, Yohanan Friedmann, J.M.S. Baljon, Simon Digby, and Muzaffar Alam have written essays that are included in this anthology. They offer opposing views in the discussion of Sufis' political activism and their interactions with the ruling class and the government. 
Edward Gait’s A History Of Assam represents a chronological history of Assam. The author discusses about the Aryans, and the later invaders like the Huns, Mughals, Pathans etc. It also traces about the prevalent religions in Assam and its practices.
Nile Green’sIndianSufism Since TheSeventeenth Century presents a study of Islamic sainthood in the context of a cosmopolitan Indian culture which is made up of migrants, warriors, writers, and princesuncovering the politics and beauty of Indian Sufism. The study focuses on the tombs of four Sufi saints of the Deccan region and their changing roles under the authority of the Mughals, the Nizams of Hyderabad, and after 1947 in the  Indian Union, placing the mystical traditions of Indian Islam in their cultural contexts. The book's emphasis on the religion of princely Hyderabad and its examination of the dynamic intellectual and cultural history of this sovereign kingdom are particularly fascinating. But careful consideration should be accorded to how the book provides an informed understanding of India as a whole, focusing primarily on the north and making no study of the North-east, howsoever.
S. L. Baruah’s article The Muslim Population in Pre-British Assam describes how the neo-Vaishnavism promoted by SrimantaSankardeva and his famous pupil, Sri Madhavdeva in  the 16th century and the teachings of Azan Fakir in the 17th century contributed to the creation of a harmonious brotherhood between Assamese Hindus and Muslims.
SwargadeoRajeshwar Singha edited by Dr. Surya Kumar Bhuyan provides an insightful knowledgeabout the chronicles of the Ahom Swargadeo Rajeshwar Singha (1751-1769) and a few other Ahom kings of late medieval Assam. The last chapter of the book, “AXOMIYA MUSALMAN” provides many information about the tolerance and devotion shown by the Ahom kings to every religion practised in contemporary Assam.It gives vital informationabout the  important positions in administration which were allotted to the Muslim subjects and the relationship shared between the Muslim saints  and the Ahom  kings as well as the mutual trust that existedamong all the subjects regardless of their beliefs. The book enlightens us with a few Zikirs composed by the Syed Sufi Saint Azan Fakir.
Hazrat Azan PeerorZigirAru Bisaar  has been  written by a profound committee member of the Azan Peer Dargah namely Syed Hakikur Rahman. The writer delivers essential information regardingthe transformation of the termZikirinto colloquial Zigirin upper Assam, especially inSivasagar district. The book is in the Assamese language and is embodied with valued Zikirs, Baulsand other compositions made by Azan Fakir.

Azan Fakir and the importance of Zikir in Assamese Sufi Literature
Azan Fakir was a Sufi saint.  It seems that he travelled from Baghdad to Assam, where he sowed the seeds of communal harmony among its indigenous communities. His life story, however, is not a fully documented one. There is a paucity of evidence and indisputable factual information. Still, it is believed that Azan Fakir was born in Baghdad, Iraq, in 1610 CE. Syed Moin-uddin Shah washis original name. The saint eventually gained fame as Shah Miran, Shah Milan, Ajan Fakir, and Ajan Pir. Syed Aush Jahan was his father's name, and there is no information available about his mother. One of Syed Aush Jahan's two sons was Ajan Fakir, and the other was Nabi Pir, also known as Nabik Uddin. According to the family genealogy, Aush Jahan hailed from the 42nd generation of Hazarat Muhhammad. Around the middle of the seventeenth century CE, Azan Fakir's active years began and the Pir started composing majority of his devotional songs called Zikirs, which have had handed down from one generation to the next orally.
Zikir, is a particular kind of Islamic meditation in which words or prayers are uttered/recited frequently to remind oneself of God. It is crucial to Sufi Islam. Zikirs in Assam are influenced greatly by Vaishnava literature, and they often use Assamese idioms.In accordance with Assamese Vaishnava poetic style, they represent the Islamic teachings.The writings of Sufi saints have assisted Assamese Muslims in understanding Islamic philosophy, the Quran, and the Hadith, just the same way the compositions of thetwo great Assamese Vaishnava pontiffs, SrimantaSankardeva and Sri Madhavadevahave made it easier for Assamese Hindu people to grasp the philosophy therein .

The Zikir by Azan Fakir reflects on the mysterious ways of the Almighty and it is a pious plea for divine help. It illustrates the calamities caused by nature, which is a reflection of Almighty’s wrath and belief that these difficult tests the Almighty subjectsus to can be passed only through sincerest devotion, “O dear, take the boat to the shore” is a metaphor for submitting to the will of the Almighty and sincerely seeking his help when we face the greatest difficulties. The Zikirmight also be a reflection of the difficulties faced during the invasion of the Mughals led by Raja Man Singh of Amber which ended inthe battle of Saraighat, a marine battle fought in 1671.

Deh-Bisaar songs demand the approval of a Master in order to uncover the hidden significance of the body (Deh) and use symbolic language to describe the confined soul of an individual. Songs from Deh-Bisaar and Bengal's Baul communities appear to share a spiritual bond. They are typically sung by roaming squatters to the beat of the Dotara or Tokari, and they are not specifically related to any one Puja or ritual.

Azan Fakir discusses a lot ofthemes including greed, rivers, land, birth, and death in the poem cited above. He might have been alluding to the SaragoriChapori event where RupaiDadhora, a Muslim official of the Ahom monarch, began a plot against him. He was successful in persuading the King to believe that Azan Fakir was a Mughal spy and that he ought to be punished.In fact, commands were issued and both of the eyeswere removed from the Pir. This led to a severe catastrophic event in which the river shifted its channel of flow and drowned the entire town except for the location where Azan Fakir and his brother Nabi Pir were living at the time. As soon as the monarch saw the saint's greatness and authority, he begged for forgiveness and was awarded a plot of land in SaraguriChapori, close to Sivasagar. The Hajarat Azan Pir Dargah is still in operation there.

The mystical Baulmusic cult is said to have originated in Bengal; but it interestingly has a distinctive position in the history of world music. Musicians in the group are predominantly Muslim Sufis and Hindu Vaishnavas. They are easily recognised by their distinctive attire and musical equipment. As a result of its origin in the Sanskrit words "Vatula" and "Vyakula", "Baul" often refers to someone who is mad or possessed . Essentially, the Bauls were recusants who rejected traditional social norms to practice music as their religion. "Baul" is also the name of the folk music genre developed by this cult. The saffron robe (alkhalla), necklace made of basil (tulsi) stem beads, and single-stringed guitar (ektara) are all characteristics of a Baul singer. Bauls live on everything villagers offer in return for their music, as they travel from place to place on their own ecstasy.
         Both the Bauls and the Sufi musicians use almost similar kind of musical instruments.Bauls adhere to a philosophy free of caste, creed, and social hierarchy. The syncretism of Sufis is similar to that of Baul philosophers. Bauls are known as 'Ayul' as well. An alternative theory refers to the Ayuls as a class of Muslim prophets. As a result of this word's origins in the Arabic language, Ayul means 'extremely worried' or 'anxious' - very worried about meeting God, your beloved. As a result of joining the Baul cult, Muslims were known as Ayuls. Sometimes their madness is compared to Sufi 'Diwana' madness. The word ‘Diwana’ indicates a particular mode of behaviour. Like the Sufi, the Baul searches for the divine beloved in a humanbeing.This context can be employed to emphasise how vital assimilation and religious affiliation were carried out throughout the period .


Observations:
After a through scrutiny of  the aforementioned Ziki,Deh-Bisar and Baulsongs, it can be said that Azan Fakir was a true Sufi and he kept himself totally aloof from the arena and agendas of politics as well as from the sphere of religious conversion (conversion of local people into Islam). His compositions were universal, people belonging to every section of the society regardless of their religion, could seek relief from his Zikirs. His compositions were answers to the sorrows of people, people from every religion and not just Islam, as his mission was never to allure people into converting to Islam but enlighten people with morals and ethics. Inthe process, he contributed in developing a spirit of universal brotherhood among the masses.
        Another interesting feature of his compositions is that he paid utmost importance toNature and the Natural forces as Nature is the emanation of God. That is why Azan Fakir wants his followers to lead a simple life without any greed or lust by raising examples of how destructive natural calamities can be which according to him is the wrath of the Almighty. Pursuit of worldly pleasures by destruction of Nature and the environment is forbidden in Sufism as a Sufi follower should always worship the Nature.
The Muslim population never suffered any injustice under the Ahom Kings but as per Ahom religious policy, conversion was highly forbidden;any kind of conversion was not encouraged to take place even within the Hindu fold . Azan Fakir, as a loyal subject of his newly adopted homeland abided by the religious directives of the Ahom royal government, which is why the Ahom kings never had any problem with Azan Fakir until the unfortunate incident of SaragoriChapori as mentioned before.

The significance of Silsilah
Silsilah, in simple words, can be described as “family genealogy”. In Islamic mysticism (Sufism), the observance of ceremonial succession from the original teacher to students is known as a formal chain of spiritual descent. Typically, the line of descent is followed from Muhammad through the order's founder to the current pupil. When a pupil is initiated into the tariqah, they are said to link themselves to the chain after receiving the order's secret chant, which conveys the chain's spiritual power. They also vow allegiance to the founder and the founder's present earthly deputy. In some orders, leadership and spiritual seniority are also determined through Silsilah .

The afore-mentioned succession chart statesthat Azan Fakir was a follower and disciple of Khwaja Nizamuddin Auliya, whose tomb is located in Delhi. Azan Fakir, who travelled to Assam from Baghdad spent a brief time at Delhi in order to develop his Sufi Saint abilities. Syed Abdul Malik describes him as a preacher with profound mastery over the Qur'an, the Hadith, and Islamic philosophy.
The Zikirs say that four saints travelled to Assam to preach Islam. They were Sawal Pir, Saleh Pir, HazratKhandekar Pir, and Azan Fakir. Pir Shah Madaris also mentioned in one of his Zikirs. 
  

Syeds are believed to have descended from the Sufi saints mentioned above and reside in various parts of Assam, including Lakhimpur, Sivasagar, Nagaon, and South Kamrup. There are also a few Syed families in lower Assam that are related to these saints. The Sufi saints mentioned above were also “Syeds” and they share the same Silsilah. Azan Fakir and Nabi Pir were brothers and were mostly working together in the beginning to preach Islam.HazratKhandekar Pir and Sawal Pir, however, kept aloof and focused on their own respective works. As a result, not many people in Assam are aware of them.
Azan Fakir has made a significant contribution to Assamese Sufi literature. However, he is not mentioned in any other works or documents with the exception of an old, unpublished book that briefly references his contributions.
